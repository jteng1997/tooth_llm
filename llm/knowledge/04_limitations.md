## What these photos cannot show

An occlusal photograph looks at the biting surfaces of the teeth. A great
deal is invisible from that angle.

It cannot show the surfaces where two teeth touch each other, which is
one of the most common places for decay to start. It cannot show anything
below the gum line, or the bone and roots underneath. It cannot show the
inside of a tooth, so it cannot tell how deep any decay goes. It cannot
show the cheek-facing or tongue-facing sides of the teeth.

X-rays are what dentists use to see most of this, and no photograph can
replace them.

## What this tool is and is not

It is an early screening aid, meant to help someone notice that a dental
visit would be worthwhile. It is not a diagnosis, it is not a substitute
for a dental examination, and a result saying nothing was found does not
mean nothing is wrong.

## The tool can also be wrong

The image analysis can miss real decay, and it can flag something that
turns out to be a stain, a shadow, a food particle or an existing
filling. Both kinds of mistake happen. Anything flagged here needs a
dentist to confirm it.

## Getting a usable photo

The photo needs to be sharp, well lit, and to include the whole arch from
the front teeth to the back molars. Teeth should be reasonably clean and
dry, since food debris and saliva glare hide surfaces.

Common problems are blur from moving while shooting, the back molars
being cut off at the edge of the frame, a dark photo with no flash, and
bright reflections washing out the biting surfaces.

If the analysis reports the photo is unusable, the honest answer is that
it cannot be assessed and needs retaking. Do not guess from a bad photo.
