## The basics that matter most

Brushing twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste is the single most useful
habit. Fluoride helps the tooth surface resist and partly recover from
acid attacks.

Spitting out toothpaste without rinsing with water afterwards leaves more
fluoride in contact with the teeth.

Cleaning between the teeth daily, with floss or interdental brushes,
reaches the surfaces a brush cannot. These are exactly the surfaces an
occlusal photo cannot see.

## Diet

How often sugar is eaten matters more than how much is eaten at once.
Each sugary snack or drink starts an acid attack that lasts a while, so
frequent snacking and sipping sweet drinks through the day is harder on
teeth than the same amount taken at a meal.

Sticky sweets and sugary drinks sipped slowly are particularly harsh.

## The back teeth specifically

Molars have deep grooves on their biting surfaces where a toothbrush
bristle cannot reach, which is why decay often starts there. Brushing the
chewing surfaces deliberately, not just the outer faces, helps.

Dentists can apply a thin protective coating called a fissure sealant
into those grooves. This is common for children and teenagers and is
worth asking about if decay keeps appearing on back teeth.

## Checkups

Most people are advised to have a checkup every six to twelve months. The
right interval depends on individual risk, and a dentist will suggest a
suitable one. People who have had several cavities generally need to be
seen more often.

## If decay keeps coming back

Repeated decay usually means something in the daily pattern is driving
it: snacking frequency, sugary drinks, a dry mouth from medication, or
cleaning that is missing certain areas. A dentist can assess individual
risk and suggest stronger fluoride or other measures.
