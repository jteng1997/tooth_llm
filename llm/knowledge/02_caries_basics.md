## What a cavity is

Tooth decay happens when bacteria in the mouth feed on sugars and produce
acid. The acid slowly dissolves minerals out of the hard outer shell of
the tooth. If this keeps happening faster than the tooth can repair
itself, a hole eventually forms. That hole is a cavity.

Decay is not something that appears overnight. It usually builds up over
months or years, which is why regular checks catch it early.

## The stages, roughly

At the earliest stage the surface has lost minerals but is still whole.
This often looks like a chalky white patch or a brown stain, and at this
point the damage can sometimes be stopped or even partly reversed with
fluoride and better cleaning.

Next, the surface breaks down and a small hole appears in the outer
enamel layer. The tooth cannot repair this by itself any more, but the
repair a dentist does is usually small.

If it goes deeper it reaches the softer layer underneath, which decays
faster. This is often when a tooth starts to feel sensitive.

Deeper still, the decay approaches the nerve in the middle of the tooth.
This is where pain usually becomes stronger and longer-lasting, and where
treatment becomes more involved.

## What treatment usually involves

Very early damage is often managed without drilling, using fluoride and
changes to cleaning and diet, with the dentist watching it over time.

A small or medium cavity is usually cleaned out and filled.

Deep decay that has reached the nerve may need root canal treatment,
where the inside of the tooth is cleaned out, and often a crown
afterwards to protect what remains.

A tooth too damaged to save may need to be removed.

Which of these applies can only be decided by a dentist examining the
tooth directly, usually with X-rays. A photograph cannot tell how deep
decay goes.

## Why earlier is easier

The earlier decay is found, the smaller and cheaper the treatment, and
the more of the natural tooth is kept. Decay does not heal on its own
once a hole has formed.
